We begin with a Gaussian input field amplitude in the near field:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{r}|^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}\right).
\end{equation}

Its Fourier transform is also a Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}\{A_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{r})\}(\mathbf{k}) 
    = \exp\!\left(-\frac{w_{0}^{2}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{4}\right).
\end{equation}

---

Now, if the input field is shifted by $\mathbf{r}_{0}$, this corresponds to convolution with a Dirac delta:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{in}}'(\mathbf{r}) 
    = \big( A_{\text{in}} \ast \delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}) \big)(\mathbf{r})
    = A_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}).
\end{equation}

In the Fourier plane this results in multiplication by a linear (blazing) phase:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}\{A_{\text{in}}'(\mathbf{r})\}(\mathbf{k})
    = \mathcal{F}\{A_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{r})\}(\mathbf{k}) \,
      e^{-i \mathbf{k}\cdot \mathbf{r}_{0}}.
\end{equation}

---

Next, we apply a phase mask corresponding to a bottle beam:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{mask}}(\mathbf{r}) 
    = A_{\text{in}}'(\mathbf{r}) \, e^{i \phi_{\text{bb}}(\mathbf{r})}.
\end{equation}

The Fourier transform of this field is given by a convolution:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}\{A_{\text{mask}}(\mathbf{r})\}(\mathbf{k})
    = \bigg[ \mathcal{F}\{A_{\text{in}}'(\mathbf{r})\}(\mathbf{k}) \bigg]
      \ast \bigg[ \mathcal{F}\{e^{i \phi_{\text{bb}}(\mathbf{r})}\}(\mathbf{k}) \bigg].
\end{equation}

---

Thus, the final output field in the Fourier plane can be written as
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{k}) 
    = \Big[ G(\mathbf{k}) \, e^{-i \mathbf{k}\cdot \mathbf{r}_{0}} \Big]
      \ast B(\mathbf{k}),
\end{equation}
where 
\[
    G(\mathbf{k}) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{w_{0}^{2}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{4}\right), 
    \quad 
    B(\mathbf{k}) = \mathcal{F}\{ e^{i \phi_{\text{bb}}(\mathbf{r})} \}.
\]

In words: the output is the Gaussian spot (shifted by a blazing phase) convolved with the Fourier representation of the bottle-beam mask.
